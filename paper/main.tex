\title{How much does a tracker-seeded ground truth favour its seed?\\
Measuring seed bias in multi-object tracking benchmarks}

% Author list, affiliations and e-mails to be confirmed by the authors.
\author[uef]{Trong Nghia Le}
\author[uef]{Thien Bao Nguyen\corref{cor}}
\cortext[cor]{Corresponding author.}
\affiliation[uef]{organization={3ITech, College of Technology and Design, University of Economics Ho Chi Minh City (UEH)},
  city={Ho Chi Minh City}, country={Vietnam}}

\begin{abstract}
Ground truth for multi-object tracking is increasingly produced by correcting the output of a tracker
rather than by annotating from scratch. Such ground truth inherits the seed tracker's errors that the
reviewer leaves uncorrected, and these errors favour the seed when the same ground truth is used for
evaluation. We quantify this seed bias. A parametric reviewer model (recall of missed objects, retention of
false positives, probability of fixing an identity error, retention of seed geometry) turns the output
of a seed tracker and the public ground truth of three benchmarks (MOT17, DanceTrack and the traffic
benchmark FastTrack) into simulated reviewed ground truth. Across eight trackers, under a
pre-registered protocol and a reference reviewer, the self-seed advantage in IDF1 is
0.069 [95\% CI 0.060, 0.089] on MOT17, 0.160 [0.148, 0.170] on DanceTrack and 0.049 [0.042, 0.059]
on FastTrack, larger than our pre-registered threshold of 0.01 on every benchmark. The advantage is
driven by unfixed identity errors: it falls to 0.005--0.018 when every identity error is fixed.
Errors correlated with the seed reorder trackers more than random errors of the same size.
A majority-consensus seed of four trackers reduces the distortion of pairwise IDF1 differences by
0.018--0.059, but a tracker outside the consensus can still lose up to 0.086 IDF1, and a bracket over
single-seed ground truths never contained the true score. Code and the full protocol are released.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}

Annotating identities for multi-object tracking (MOT) frame by frame is expensive, so ground truth
(GT) is now commonly produced by running a tracker, or a detector with propagation, and asking people
to correct the result. The procedure is efficient, and tracker-generated labels have been shown to be
useful for training at a fraction of the cost \cite{cetintas2024spam}. When the corrected output is
also used to \emph{evaluate} trackers, however, every error that the reviewer leaves in place is an
error that the seed tracker shares, and the seed is rewarded for it.

The mechanism is known in other fields. In dependency parsing, gold standards produced by correcting
parser output score the seeding parser 5.8--6.9 points higher than gold annotated from scratch
\cite{berzak2016anchoring}; with model-assisted annotation of subjective tasks, reported model
performance increases when the assisted labels are used as gold \cite{schroeder2025human}. Diagnostic
accuracy research calls the effect incorporation bias and checks for it routinely \cite{whiting2011quadas2}.
In MOT, the DIVOTrack authors noted that GT pre-generated by a tracker gives that tracker an unfair
advantage and mitigated it by full review \cite{hao2023divotrack}, but we are not aware of any
measurement of the effect, of its dependence on how careful the reviewer is, or of protocols that
remove it. Label errors are known to change scores, and sometimes rankings, on detection and
classification benchmarks \cite{northcutt2021pervasive,singh2024cocorem,penquitt2026incomplete},
where errors are usually treated as independent of the evaluated models. Seed bias is different: the
errors are correlated with one of the competitors by construction.

We ask three questions. (RQ1) How large is the advantage a seed tracker gains on GT built from its own
output? (RQ2) Do seed-correlated GT errors reorder trackers more than random errors of the same size?
(RQ3) Which inexpensive annotation protocols reduce the bias? We answer them by simulation on
public benchmarks whose complete GT serves as the truth, using a reviewer model with interpretable
parameters, eight trackers and three benchmarks, under a protocol that was fixed before any result was
computed. Our contributions are: (i) a quantitative measurement of seed bias in MOT, which is large
relative to differences between current trackers; (ii) the finding that it is driven by uncorrected
identity errors rather than by box geometry or missed objects; (iii) evidence that seed-correlated
errors distort rankings more than random errors; and (iv) an evaluation of consensus seeding and
bracketing as mitigations, including two negative results.

\section{Related work}
\label{sec:related}

\textbf{Label errors and benchmark rankings.} Test-set label errors are common and can change
model rankings \cite{northcutt2021pervasive}. Re-annotating COCO masks changes the relative standing of
detectors at high IoU \cite{singh2024cocorem}, while a recent re-annotation of detection benchmarks
reports that scores are sensitive to missing boxes but rankings remain largely stable
\cite{penquitt2026incomplete}. These studies consider errors that are not tied to a particular
evaluated model; RQ2 asks whether errors tied to one model behave differently.

\textbf{Incomplete and assisted labels in tracking.} TETA addresses evaluation under incomplete
annotation \cite{li2022teta}, and SPAM shows that tracker-generated pseudo-labels with targeted
human effort train strong trackers \cite{cetintas2024spam}. Neither analyses what happens when
assisted labels are the reference for evaluation.

\textbf{Pre-annotation bias.} Pre-annotation speeds up annotation and can bias it toward the
pre-annotating system, with effect sizes that vary by task
\cite{lingren2014preannotation,berzak2016anchoring,schroeder2025human}.

\textbf{Metrics.} We use IDF1 \cite{ristani2016idf1} as the primary metric, HOTA \cite{luiten2021hota}
and CLEAR MOT \cite{bernardin2008clear} as secondary metrics, all computed with the official TrackEval
implementation \cite{luiten2020trackeval}.

\section{Seeded ground truth and the reviewer model}
\label{sec:model}

Let $\gtt$ be complete ground truth and $A$ a seed tracker. A reviewer with parameters
$\theta = (r, p_{fp}, q, a)$ edits the output of $A$ into simulated reviewed ground truth $\mathrm{GT}_A(\theta)$:
\begin{itemize}
\item seed boxes are matched to $\gtt$ frame by frame (Hungarian assignment, IoU $\ge 0.5$);
\item each run of consecutive missed GT boxes of one object is added with probability $r$;
\item each run of consecutive unmatched seed boxes of one seed identity is kept with probability $p_{fp}$; an added
GT box replaces an overlapping (IoU $\ge 0.1$) unmatched seed box;
\item seed identities are matched one-to-one to GT identities as in IDF1; a pair (seed id, GT id) outside
this matching is an identity error, and errors whose corrections interact (e.g. the two halves of a swap)
form one \emph{episode}, which is fixed with probability $q$;
\item a matched box keeps the seed geometry with probability $a$.
\end{itemize}
With $(r, p_{fp}, q, a) = (1, 0, 1, 0)$ the reviewer reproduces $\gtt$; with $(0, 1, 0, 1)$ it returns the
seed output unchanged. Both identities, and the fix probability per episode type, are covered by tests.

For a metric $M$ (IDF1 unless stated), the \emph{self-seed advantage} of $A$ over another tracker $B$ is
\begin{equation}
\begin{split}
\SA(A,B) = {} & \bigl[M(A;\mathrm{GT}_A) - M(B;\mathrm{GT}_A)\bigr] \\
 & - \bigl[M(A;\gtt) - M(B;\gtt)\bigr],
\end{split}
\end{equation}
the change in $A$'s lead over $B$ caused by seeding. Because the reviewer only ever moves GT toward the seed,
$\SA \ge 0$ follows from the model; the empirical content is its \emph{magnitude}. We therefore test
$\SA \ge \delta$ with $\delta = 0.01$ IDF1.

\section{Experimental setup}
\label{sec:setup}

\textbf{Data.} We use the public GT of MOT17 (seven training sequences) \cite{milan2016mot16},
DanceTrack (25 validation sequences) \cite{sun2022dancetrack} and FastTrack (12 urban traffic CCTV
videos, nine classes) \cite{hashempoor2025fasttracker}. FastTrack classes are mapped to six evaluation
classes (person, bus, truck, car, bicycle, motorbike); its ignore regions and minor classes are treated as
ignore regions. MOT17 follows TrackEval's preprocessing (distractor classes removed).

\textbf{Trackers.} Detections come from COCO-pretrained YOLO11x and YOLO11n \cite{ultralytics2024yolo11}
(input 1280 px, confidence $\ge 0.05$), without fine-tuning. Four trackers -- SORT \cite{bewley2016sort},
ByteTrack \cite{zhang2022bytetrack}, OC-SORT \cite{cao2023ocsort} and BoT-SORT without camera-motion
compensation \cite{aharon2022botsort} -- run with library defaults \cite{roboflow2025trackers}, giving
eight systems. The four YOLO11x systems serve as seeds. Because all trackers pass detector boxes through,
systems that share a detector output identical geometry.

\textbf{Reviewer settings.} The reference reviewer is $\theta_{ref} = (0.75, 0.1, 0.5, 1)$, an assumption
rather than a measurement; results are also reported over a grid of 192 settings
($r \in \{0, .25, .5, .75, .9, 1\}$, $p_{fp} \in \{0, .1, .25, .5\}$, $q \in \{0, .5, .9, 1\}$, $a \in \{0, 1\}$)
with ten random draws each, using common random numbers across settings.

\textbf{Statistics.} IDF1 is pooled over all (sequence, class) units of a benchmark. Intervals are 95\% BCa
\cite{efron1987bca} over 10,000 bootstrap resamples of sequences, with jackknife acceleration. Confirmatory
statements are made only at $\theta_{ref}$ and per benchmark; the grid is descriptive.

\textbf{Pre-registration.} Hypotheses, endpoints, the grid, analysis rules and a stopping rule were frozen
before any experiment; seven dated amendments, each made before any result was computed, are part of the
released protocol.

Table~\ref{tab:h1} reports the advantage. On every benchmark the lower confidence bound exceeds
$\delta = 0.01$ several times over, so the confirmatory hypothesis holds on all three. The advantage is of
the same order as, or larger than, the spread between the evaluated trackers on the true GT
(e.g. 0.509--0.535 IDF1 for three of the four YOLO11x systems on MOT17). It is largest on DanceTrack,
where uniform appearance and diverse motion produce many identity errors, and smallest on FastTrack. It
is not an artefact of one tracker: without pairs involving SORT it remains 0.039--0.145, and with SORT
fed every detection it is 0.058--0.173. Changing the matching threshold used by the reviewer from 0.5 to
0.4 or 0.6 changes it by less than 0.01. On secondary metrics the advantage is 0.042--0.140 in
HOTA$_{0.5}$ and is concentrated in association accuracy (AssA$_{0.5}$: 0.060--0.196) rather than
detection accuracy (DetA$_{0.5}$: 0.025--0.030).

\centering
\includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{figures/fig_sa_curves.pdf}
\caption{Self-seed advantage as one reviewer parameter varies with the others at $\theta_{ref}$
(shaded: BCa 95\%). (a) Recall of missed objects $r$. (b) Probability $q$ of fixing an identity-error

\label{fig:curves}

\textbf{What drives the advantage.} Fig.~\ref{fig:curves} varies one reviewer parameter at a time.
Fixing identity errors is decisive: at $q = 1$ the advantage drops to 0.015 (MOT17), 0.005 (DanceTrack)
and 0.018 (FastTrack), while at $q = 0$ it is 0.124, 0.327 and 0.079. Adding every missed object
($r = 1$) leaves 0.030--0.153, and the retention of false positives changes it by at most 0.013 over the
range $p_{fp} \in [0, 0.5]$. Box geometry does not contribute: with a shared detector the advantage
differs by less than 0.001 between $a = 1$ and $a = 0$, and against the other detector by at most 0.005. Of the 192
grid settings, 170 (MOT17), 161 (DanceTrack) and 172 (FastTrack) have a lower confidence bound above
$\delta$. On FastTrack the advantage is positive for every class (0.021 for bus to 0.079 for motorbike) and
unchanged (0.050 [0.042, 0.060]) when the two classes with more than 10\% class confusion are removed.

\subsection{RQ2: ranking distortion}

\begin{figure}[!t]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=\columnwidth]{figures/fig_h2.pdf}
\caption{Ranking distortion of seed-correlated errors relative to random errors of the same number and
size: $\tau_{null} - \tau_{seed}$ (Kendall $\tau$-b between rankings of eight systems on simulated and true
GT), with GT geometry on both sides ($a = 0$). Shaded: BCa 95\%.}
\label{fig:h2}
\end{figure}

Ranking eight systems on seeded GT at $\theta_{ref}$ reverses, on average, 3.23 (MOT17), 3.52 (DanceTrack)
and 1.02 (FastTrack) pairs whose true IDF1 differs by more than $\delta$. To separate the effect of the
\emph{correlation} with the seed from the effect of having errors at all, each seeded GT is paired with a
null GT that contains the same number and lengths of missed runs, unfixed identity errors and kept
false-positive runs, placed at random on the true GT. Seed-correlated errors reduce rank agreement more
than null errors: $\tau_{null} - \tau_{seed}$ is 0.198 [0.118, 0.350], 0.227 [0.204, 0.284] and
0.104 [0.039, 0.161], and they cause 2.35 [1.02, 4.08], 3.17 [2.98, 3.97] and 1.05 [0.28, 2.00] more
reversals (Fig.~\ref{fig:h2}). Two limitations qualify the comparison. The null could not place 12--14\%
of the false-positive boxes on DanceTrack and FastTrack, and 25--50\% of the kept false-positive boxes in
seeded GT carry the label of a real track, an error type the null does not reproduce; both make the
null somewhat milder than the seeded GT.

\textbf{Union seeding is not a mitigation} when trackers share a detector: because the trackers pass
detector boxes through, the union of their outputs, de-duplicated by IoU, equals the output of the first
member, so union seeding reduces to single seeding.

\textbf{Consensus seeding} seeds only boxes supported by a majority of four trackers (mean geometry) and
links them only where a plurality of trackers keeps the same identity; other boxes are shown as
anonymous candidates. It reduces the distortion $D$ on all three benchmarks (Table~\ref{tab:h3}), at the
price of 14--35\% more boxes to verify. The reduction does not transfer fully to trackers outside the
consensus: when one tracker is left out of the consensus, the remaining members gain up to 0.086 IDF1
(DanceTrack) over it, and on DanceTrack leaving out BoT-SORT or ByteTrack removes most of the reduction in
$D$.

\textbf{Bracketing}, i.e. reporting the range of a tracker's scores over GTs seeded by different trackers,
never contained the true score: all seeded GTs share the errors that no tracker resolves, so they
are biased in the same direction.

\section{Discussion}
\label{sec:discussion}

Seed bias is large enough to change conclusions: at the reference reviewer it exceeds every pairwise
IDF1 difference between the YOLO11x systems on MOT17 and DanceTrack, and half of them on FastTrack. Its source is identity: reviewers who fix every identity error
remove most of it, whereas adding missed objects or deleting false positives helps much less. For
benchmark builders this suggests that review effort is best spent on identity continuity, that the
seeding tracker should be disclosed and excluded from comparisons on its own GT, and that consensus
seeding helps only the trackers that took part in it.

\textbf{Limitations.} The reviewer is simulated, and $\theta_{ref}$ is an assumption; the grid shows how
conclusions depend on it, and a study measuring real reviewers' parameters would calibrate it. All
trackers are motion-based and share detectors; trackers with appearance models or different detectors
may produce less correlated errors. MOT17 has only seven sequences, so its intervals are approximate.
The null model of RQ2 is somewhat milder than the seeded GT (Section~\ref{sec:results}).

\section{Conclusion}

Ground truth built by correcting a tracker's output favours that tracker by 0.05--0.16 IDF1 under a
plausible reviewer on three public benchmarks, mainly through identity errors left uncorrected, and
seed-correlated errors reorder trackers more than random errors. Benchmarks built with tracker
assistance should report the seed, prioritise identity review, and avoid comparing the seed on its own
ground truth.
